# Design — Campus Customs

I wanted the site to feel like the Broadway shop rather than a generic store
template. Everything below is a deliberate choice, not a default.

## Type

**Fraunces** for headlines and prices, **Inter** for everything functional.

Fraunces is a serif with a bit of character — it reads collegiate and old New
Haven without tipping into a fake varsity font. Using it for prices as well as
headlines is the trick: the price stops looking like form data and starts
looking like part of the brand.

*Why it helps:* a shopper can tell within a second that this is a real shop with
a point of view, not a dropshipper. That first impression is what decides
whether they scroll.

## Colour

Yale Blue `#00356B` stays the anchor, but the page sits on **parchment**
`#F6F3EC` instead of white, with a **brass** `#B08D33` accent for rules,
eyebrow labels and the shield.

*Why it helps:* every competitor is navy-on-white. The warm field makes our
product photos — almost all navy and gray garments — read warmer and more
premium, and it stops 102 products on one page from feeling clinical. Brass is
used only in small doses, so it signals "detail worth reading" rather than
decoration.

## Hierarchy

Each section now leads with a small brass eyebrow, then a serif title, over a
blue rule. The hero carries four facts — 102 pieces, XS–XXL, 57 Broadway, open
7 days.

*Why it helps:* the eyebrow tells you what kind of thing you are looking at
before you read the heading, so scanning is faster. The hero facts answer the
two questions a new visitor actually has — is there enough here, and is this a
real shop — without making them hunt.

## Motion

Sections and cards fade up as they enter view, slightly staggered. Cards lift
on hover. The hero underline draws itself once. The chat panel scales up from
its corner. All of it is disabled for anyone with reduced motion turned on.

*Why it helps:* the stagger makes a long grid feel like it is being laid out
for you rather than dumped. The hover lift tells you a card is clickable before
you click. Nothing moves that is not telling you something — I deliberately
avoided animation that just decorates, because on a shopping page that reads as
slow.

## Product presentation

Cards are 4:5 on white tiles, with the product name in serif, colour shown as
actual **colour dots** instead of words, and the **size run sliding up on
hover** — in-stock sizes solid, sold-out ones struck through.

*Why it helps:* this is the change I think earns the most. Our data has 145
size-rows at zero, so a quarter of the time a shopper picks a size that is not
there. Showing the real size run on the card moves that disappointment to
before the click. Colour dots do the same job for colourways — "navy blue,
heather gray, white" is three words to read, or one glance.

## Chat

The assistant has a brass shield avatar, a live status dot, and four suggested
openers ("What shirts do you have?", "Something for The Game"). Replies animate
in, product cards sit inline, and the launcher pulses once in a while.

*Why it helps:* an empty chat box is a blank page problem — most people close
it. The suggested openers give a first move, and they are written to show what
the assistant is actually good at, which is finding things in the catalogue.
The avatar and status dot make it read as part of the shop rather than a bolted
on widget.

---

## The business case, short

Two things keep someone on a shop site: believing it is real, and not hitting
dead ends. The type, colour and hierarchy do the first. The size run on cards,
the colour dots and the chat openers do the second — each one removes a click
that would otherwise end in "not in my size" or "I don't know what to ask".
